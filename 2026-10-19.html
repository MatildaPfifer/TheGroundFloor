The short version

Mortgage rates finally stopped climbing, but a hot inflation report means they may not get relief soon. And in a surprise for a market known for empty offices, Houston developers are pitching new skyscrapers again.

Two tower plans surfaced this week: a 32-story building on Westheimer near River Oaks District, and a downtown block on Buffalo Bayou Park being marketed for a next-generation high-rise. The logic is that Houston has plenty of vacant office space overall, but very little in new, top-tier buildings. Downtown towers built since 2010 are about 95% occupied.

Housing is moving the other way. Houston sales fell 5.7% in September as inventory kept rising, and nationally, Redfin says pending sales hit their lowest level since 2014 while contract cancellations reached a near record. With inflation at 3.7%, markets now see close to even odds that the Fed raises rates again on October 28.

HousingOct 15

Houston home sales fall 5.7% as rates top 7%

The Houston Association of Realtors counted 7,030 single-family sales in September. The median price held at $325,000, while inventory rose 6.3% to 40,192 homes, or about 5.5 months of supply. The monthly payment on a median-priced home rose to about $1,705 from $1,622 a year ago, and condo and townhome closings dropped 21%.

Mortgage rates hold at 7.40%, ending a seven-week climb

Freddie Mac's 30-year average was unchanged for the week, and the 15-year eased to 6.71%. A year ago the 30-year averaged 6.27%. Rates are holding at their highest level since late 2023.

EconomyOct 14

A hot inflation report raises the odds of another Fed hike

Consumer prices rose 0.6% in September and 3.7% over the past year, above the 3.6% forecast. Core inflation came in at 2.6%. After the report, markets priced roughly a 47% chance of a rate hike in October, up from 21% before. It was the last major inflation reading before the Fed meets.

HousingOct 19

U.S. pending home sales hit their lowest level since 2014

Redfin says pending sales fell in all 50 of the most populous metros for the first time in three years, and 14.5% of September contracts were canceled, the highest share outside early 2020. Inventory is at its highest since March 2020. San Antonio ranked among the metros with the most canceled deals.

The City of Houston is marketing its 1968 office building at 611 Walker, a prime block on the west side of downtown with clear views of Buffalo Bayou Park, as a site for the city's next skyscraper. Cushman & Wakefield's Tim Relyea is representing the city and has teamed up with architect Pickard, whose Houston work includes the ExxonMobil campus, 609 Main and the Amegy Bank building.

The lesson is that Houston's office market is really two markets. Older buildings are fighting over tenants with discounts and free rent, while the newest towers are nearly full. That is why a city with high overall vacancy can still justify new construction. The catch is that a new tower needs an anchor tenant and financing, and both are harder to land with rates where they are.

Realty News Report

Underwriting desk: reading a split office market

When someone says office is dead or office is back, both can be true. Here is how to read the market before you underwrite a new tower:

  1. Ignore the headline vacancy rate. Split it by building age and class, since a 1980s tower and a 2020 tower are not competing for the same tenants.
  2. Count the large blocks. How many buildings can offer a big tenant 100,000 square feet or more of contiguous space in a top building? If the answer is very few, there is a real gap.
  3. Trace where tenants come from. Most new tower tenants move from older buildings nearby, which leaves vacancy behind. Ask who backfills that space.
  4. Check the preleasing bar. Lenders usually want a meaningful share of the building signed before construction starts, so the anchor tenant often decides whether the project happens at all.

The best office deals right now are not about the market. They are about the building and who is moving into it.
