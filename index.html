The short version

Houston home sales had their slowest October in a decade, but developers are still buying land and breaking ground for the long run.

Mortgage rates fell for a fourth straight week to 7.11%, yet a hotter than expected inflation report makes a December Fed hike more likely. Houston sales fell 14.9% in October, and the median price slipped to its lowest level since January 2024. At the same time, Brookfield Residential bought 476 acres in City Place for homes that will not go on sale until 2028, and two big industrial projects broke ground in north and west Houston.

This week also takes on a question from our group chat: is most of today's deal volume coming from distressed sales? The data says distress is rising quickly, especially for apartments, but it is still a small share of total sales. Both views can be true at once, and the underwriting desk explains why.

Houston

HousingNov 11

Houston has its slowest October for home sales in 10 years

HAR counted 6,348 single-family sales, down 14.9% from a year earlier, though sales for the year so far are still within 2% of 2025. The median price fell 3% to $320,000, the lowest since January 2024, while the average held at $421,100. Listings rose 6.8% to 40,253, and inventory stayed at 5.5 months, tied for the highest since 2012. Townhome and condo sales dropped 23.5%.

Beyond Houston

Capital marketsNov 12

Mortgage rates fall for a fourth straight week to 7.11%

Freddie Mac's 30-year average dropped from 7.18% to its lowest level since late September, and the 15-year fell to 6.45%. A year ago the 30-year averaged 6.24%. Rates had been rising since late February, when the conflict with Iran pushed up oil prices and inflation, and they could stay above 7% for some time.

EconomyNov 10

Inflation runs hotter than expected before the Fed meets

Consumer prices rose 0.4% in October against forecasts of 0.3%, and core prices rose 0.3% against 0.2%. Shelter costs drove more than 30% of the increase. There is no annual figure this month because prices were not collected during last year's government shutdown. Combined with the strong jobs report, the data supports expectations for a December rate hike.

DistressThrough Q3

Distressed sales are up 26%, led by apartments

MSCI counted $13.2 billion of distressed commercial sales from January through September, up 26.4% from a year earlier. Even so, distress made up just 2.4% of total sales, and only 1.5% in the third quarter. Apartments were the only sector where distressed sales grew faster than normal sales, up 62.8%. Debt funds are refinancing many troubled owners, which keeps more distress from reaching the sales market.

The Real Deal

DebtNov 3

Office loan delinquencies sit near a record

Trepp's overall CMBS delinquency rate dipped to 7.87% in October, but office loans were 12.33% delinquent, just below their all-time high, compared with 1.10% for industrial. Multifamily rose to 8.09% and retail jumped to 7.21%. The tenfold gap between office and industrial explains why investors keep favoring warehouses.

West of Houston along Interstate 10, MetLife Investment Management and Panattoni are building a second phase at Enterprise Park at Twinwood after the first phase fully leased to Niedex and Vestas, one of the world's largest wind turbine makers. Phase II adds three buildings from 91,000 to 181,000 square feet with 32-foot clear heights and outdoor storage. Nearby, Clay Development is building 552,240 square feet across two spec buildings, and Bridge Logistics bought Clay's 767,520-square-foot Twinwood Distribution Center III, leased to Crane Worldwide Logistics, the company led by Astros owner Jim Crane.

The lesson is how developers manage risk when money is expensive. MetLife and Panattoni waited until Phase I was full before starting Phase II, which proves demand before more capital goes in. Formation is taking the opposite approach at the Grand Parkway, building all six buildings at once because there is so little space north of FM 1960. Both can work, but the phased approach is the safer one in a 7% world.

Realty News Report

Underwriting desk: how much of the market is distress?

A great debate came up in our group chat: members who work in the industry said equity is incredibly hard to raise and most deal volume they see is distressed. The national data says distress is only 2.4% of sales. Here is how both can be true:

  1. Volume and share are different. Distressed sales are up 26%, which feels like a lot on the ground, but total sales grew even faster, so the share stays small.
  2. It depends on the sector. Apartment distress is up 62.8%, and office loans are over 12% delinquent. If you work in those sectors, distress is a much bigger part of your world than the national average suggests.
  3. A lot of distress never shows up as a sale. Owners and lenders often extend or modify loans quietly, and debt funds refinance troubled owners. Those deals do not count as distressed sales, but the stress is still there.
  4. Hard equity changes who wins. When investors are cautious, the deals that close are the ones with clear cash flow or a motivated seller, which is why distressed and stabilized assets dominate the headlines.

The takeaway is that the market is not frozen, but it is selective. Money is moving toward safe income and discounted problem assets, and away from everything in between.
