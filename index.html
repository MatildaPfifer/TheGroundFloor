The short version

Mortgage rates rose for a seventh straight week to 7.40%, the highest since November 2023, and Houston developers kept breaking ground on warehouses anyway.

Industrial is the clearest bet in town right now. Portman started a 714,233-square-foot park near Bush Intercontinental Airport, a new spec building is planned just outside Loop 610 in south Houston, and a Fort Bend County building traded for $26.8 million. Developers are building without tenants signed because they expect demand to be there when the buildings deliver in 2027.

The other big local story is a comeback attempt. A Sugar Land developer closed on 120 acres at Highway 288 and Beltway 8 in Pearland, a corner where two earlier developers tried and failed to build. Nationally, Ken Griffin paid a record $1.1 billion for land in Miami's Wynwood, and Los Angeles office leasing had its best quarter since 2019.

Sugar Land-based Planned Community Developers closed on the land at the southwest corner of Highway 288 and Beltway 8 after Pearland City Council approved the project. Plans also call for between 150,000 and 300,000 square feet of Class A office, a hotel and conference center, and waterfront restaurants. CBRE will lead retail leasing and JLL will lead office leasing.

The backstory is the lesson. PCD is the third developer to put this corner under contract. NewQuest once considered a $350 million project here, and Poag & McEwen bought the land decades ago for a $150 million outdoor shopping center that never got built. This time the deal structure is different: the city requires commercial development before all of the homes can be built, and Pearland would reimburse the developer for the conference center only after the project hits agreed performance benchmarks. When a great site keeps failing, the fix is often in how the deal is structured, not in the site itself.

Underwriting desk: how to read a spec industrial deal

Three Houston industrial projects made news this week, and two are being built without a tenant. Before you believe a spec deal pencils, check these four things:

  1. Lease-up time. How many months does the model assume before the building is full, and how much free rent will it take to get there?
  2. Building fit. Does the size, depth and loading match what tenants in that submarket actually lease? Gateway 1960 offers cross-dock, front-load and rear-load options for exactly this reason.
  3. Carry cost. Every extra month of vacancy is another month of construction loan interest, and at today's rates that adds up fast.
  4. Competing supply. Count the other spec buildings delivering nearby at the same time, since they will be chasing the same tenants.

A spec building that leases quickly becomes the kind of asset investors pay up for, like the Blue Ridge sale this week. One that sits empty can eat its own returns.
